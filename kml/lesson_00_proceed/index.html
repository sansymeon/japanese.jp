Kanji・Music・Landscape

How to Study with KML

Seeing rhythm, emotion, and structure — and letting the hand remember what the eyes have seen.

Optional guidance

A quiet map for each lesson

This is not only about remembering — it is about seeing.

1. Watch the stroke story

Begin each lesson by watching the stroke-order animation. Notice how each line appears — the direction, balance, and pause between strokes. Don’t rush. Let the motion imprint itself as a rhythm, not just a shape.

2. Feel the emotion through the emoji

Every kanji in KML carries an emoji companion — a spark of emotion or imagery that connects the abstract to the familiar. Take a moment to sense what it evokes: joy, calm, danger, life, time. Memory begins where emotion starts.

3. Close your eyes — can you still “see” it?

Once you’ve watched and felt the kanji, close your eyes. Can you still see the flow of strokes? The emoji’s emotion? The story behind it? That’s where true visual memory begins to form.

4. Trace with your finger

Now trace the character slowly with your finger or stylus, following the animation’s order. Build your muscle memory step by step — not for perfection, but for fluency. The goal is for the kanji to one day flow off your hand without thought.

5. Write like a beginner, see like an artist

In the beginning, write with the focus of a first grader. It’s not childish — it’s training your hand to move with awareness. Once your form feels steady, allow natural expressiveness to emerge. You are an adult learner, which means you bring a sense of beauty and intention to every stroke.

6. One reading, naturally absorbed

Each kanji is introduced with just one key reading. Don’t force memorization — let repetition and exposure do their quiet work. Meaning first, then sound. Let language come alive naturally.

Bonus: try your stroke in the wild

After a few finger or paper repetitions, explore how it behaves outside the lesson:

  • Use a brush pen and feel the resistance of real ink.
  • Or open Google Translate, switch to Japanese input, and use the handwriting pad.
  • See if recognition succeeds — and if not, adjust balance or order until it does.

This playful feedback loop sharpens awareness of what truly defines a kanji’s identity.

7. Component names

As you move through the lessons, you will meet recurring building blocks with English names. KML often keeps names from Heisig’s Remembering the Kanji when they clearly match the shape — and occasionally simplifies a name when a simpler label is easier to recognize.

Read more: Component Names on the Learning Companions page.

8. When a kanji becomes a component

When a kanji you already know reappears inside another character, we keep its main symbol to represent its meaning or feeling — without rebuilding the whole story.

Example: 卓 (🌻＋🪵) → 朝 (🌻＋🌙)

You are not just copying symbols — you are retracing thought, rhythm, and memory left by centuries of writers before you.

When you are ready, open a book — or warm the hand with stroke practice.
